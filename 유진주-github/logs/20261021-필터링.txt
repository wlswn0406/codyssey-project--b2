Anthropic pitches AI safety bill that may block a model if 50+ people could be killed
Lucas Ropek
4:01 PM PDT · October 20, 2026
Over the past year, concerns have swirled over the potential dangers posed by artificial intelligence, and debates have raged over the proper path for AI safety. Now, Anthropic appears to have put forth its own vision for how the government might regulate its industry.
Politico
recently reported
that the AI lab has been shopping around a new draft bill on AI safety that it hopes Congress might consider adopting. The legislation posits certain scenarios in which the release of an AI model could be blocked from distribution.
Those scenarios involve a “foreseeable, imminent risk” — one in which over 50 people are at risk of dying or if over a billion dollars in property damage could result, Politico writes. The catastrophic risk in question must “stem from the creation or release of a biological weapon, the model evading its developer’s controls or a cyberattack,” the outlet notes.
The metrics offered here — 50 people and a billion dollars — are similar to those in a piece of California legislation that was recently passed into law,
Senate Bill 53
, which similarly defined those scenarios as tantamount to “catastrophic risk.”
Anthropic’s bill suggests that analysis of a model’s potential for disaster could be arrived at via the use of “independent evaluators” who would be registered with the U.S. Commerce Department. These evaluators could potentially be paid by the AI labs themselves — so long as the “payment is not contingent on the evaluations’ results,” Politico writes.
It’s not clear how this proposed system would assure that payments couldn’t influence the evaluation. Demis Hassabis, chairman of DeepMind, posited some months ago that
a self-governing AI regulatory body should
mimic the financial industry’s Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA).

Anthropic’s cloud partnerships are getting messy
Anthropic is having trouble securing enough cloud capacity from AWS, Google, and Microsoft, which increasingly see the Claude-maker as a competitor rather than a customer, according to an S-1 filing leaked to
The Wall Street Journal
.
The leaked filing provides more color to the numbers
Reuters
and the
Financial Times
reported in late September, including that Anthropic believes it could spend more $750 billion on cloud contracts, some of which includes deals still in negotiation.
And still, that is not enough. Anthropic is leasing its own data centers and working with third-party data center operators. But the firm worries those operators have a “limited operating history,” which could make them less reliable than the hyperscalers.
Anthropic’s enormous spend comes on the back of an $80.7 billion net loss in the first half of the year. The company reported $16.3 billion in revenue during that same time period, with only two customers accounting for about a fifth of sales, according to the WSJ report.
Building on previously reported catastrophic risk disclosures, the filing also warns that Anthropic could be held liable for damages caused by harmful AI behavior to a tune that would exceed available insurance coverage.
Last week, the company told prospective investors that third-quarter
revenue alone has now reached $16.8 billion
, up 45% from the previous quarter. Anthropic is
reportedly
targeting a November IPO.
Anthropic did not immediately respond to TechCrunch’s request for comment.

Tim Fernholz
Kirsten Korosec
1:40 PM PDT · October 20, 2026
SpaceX’s data centers and Grok agents were more profitable than investors expected in third quarter of the year, as the company leaves its roots as a space company behind to focus on artificial intelligence, which now makes up 52% of its income.
CEO Elon Musk’s deep tech conglomerate reported $14.6 billion in third-quarter revenue on Tuesday, according to its
regulatory filing
.
That number was driven by growth in all segments, but especially its renamed “Super Intelligence” business line, which generated $7.5 billion in revenue from the recently acquired Cursor coding tool, Grok bot, and most importantly, the company’s burgeoning neocloud business, which now boasts 1.7 GW of compute. The AI business generated $431 million in profit, which would have been higher without $1.2 billion in one-time stock-based compensation expenses related to the Cursor acquisition.
The top-line revenue number was also boosted by about $1 billion in income related to the recovery of Bitcoin prices and unspecified related-party income that might be attributed to a SpaceX investment in another company.

OpenAI acquires startup Orchestra Bio⁠ to beef up its biotech business
OpenAI on Tuesday acquired a four-year-old AI biotech software startup called Orchestra Bio, the company
announced
via its website. Terms of the deal were not disclosed, but the startup had reportedly raised
around $20 million
as of 2025 from backers like Andreessen Horowitz, Atria, and 8VC.
Orchestra creates AI agents that help biotech companies manage the minutiae of drug discovery operations, from tracking finances to resolving scheduling issues with clinical trials.
OpenAI says it’s adding Orchestra’s team and technology to work on
Rosalind
, its AI model for life sciences research, and to its related Rosalind Workbench tool, a harness that aims to help researchers plan experiments and analyze results. OpenAI has already won some big names as customers for Rosalind, including, it says,
Moderna⁠
and
Novo Nordisk⁠.
The acquisition is a classic example of how the biggest AI labs (namely OpenAI and Anthropic) are building ever more software for the biggest industries, like pharma. In the startup world, there is an ongoing fear that at any moment, the labs will launch new features and become competitors. In this case, Orchestra is getting absorbed, rather than stomped out.
